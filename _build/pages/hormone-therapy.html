Hormone levels in women can begin to shift as early as your 30s, and the change shows up in how you look, feel and perform. Any of these worth a blood test:

  • Low energy and fatigue
  • Weight gain and belly fat
  • Low libido

Why it happens

Your hormones work together toward one goal: balance

Several things can push them out of it — and when one hormone runs low, it changes your energy, hunger, sleep, mood and sexual wellbeing.

  • Stress
  • Pregnancy
  • Aging and menopause
  • Genetics
  • Underlying health issues

Alongside menopause, there are two causes worth understanding, because standard testing often misses both.

Adrenal fatigue

Your adrenal glands sit on top of your kidneys and produce about 20% of your reproductive hormones, plus cortisol, your primary stress hormone. Under sustained stress, excess cortisol can reverse the normal cortisol curve.

The result: deep sluggishness, poor sleep, lower sex drive, a slower metabolism and a weakened immune response.

Hypothyroidism

The thyroid is a butterfly-shaped gland at the base of your neck, and it drives your body’s entire metabolic function — including body temperature, hydration, and hair and nail growth.

Routine primary-care testing, and sometimes even an endocrinologist’s, can miss a sluggish thyroid. Untreated, it can drive hot flashes, muscle weakness, dehydration and weight gain.

Perimenopause and menopause

The shift starts earlier than most women expect

  1. 20s

    Your sex hormones are typically at their highest.

  2. 30s

    Some women begin to feel the hormonal shift into perimenopause — often before anyone tells them to expect it.

  3. 40s

    Hormones peak and start to drop off, and symptoms become harder to explain away.

  4. 50s

    Most women have been through menopause and no longer have a menstrual cycle.

Pregnancy and chronic conditions such as polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) can cause large hormonal swings at any age.

Hormones your body already recognizes

Bioidentical hormone therapy uses hormones that are structurally identical to the ones your body produces. They’re typically derived from plant sources and compounded to match your own hormone levels.

Unlike synthetic hormones, bioidentical hormones are nearly chemically identical to what your body makes on its own — which is why we consider them the most natural choice for hormone replacement. Types of therapy

Four ways to take it

The right method depends on your body’s needs, your routine and what you’re most comfortable with. Your provider walks you through the options and dials in the dose.

Pellet Steady release, every few months

Small bioidentical pellets are inserted into the fatty tissue just under the skin, usually at the hip or buttock. Once in place they release hormones gradually into the bloodstream, closely mimicking your body’s own production.

Injection Precise dosing, on a schedule

Injections deliver a controlled dose of estrogen, progesterone or testosterone directly. They need regular administration, but give consistent delivery and suit patients who want a hands-off routine between doses.

Cream Daily, applied at home

Topical creams carry hormones through the skin and into the bloodstream. Applied to specific areas each day, they hold steady hormone levels and are the least invasive option.

Supplement Nutraceutical support

Nutraceuticals are derived from natural sources and contain bioactive compounds that may support hormone balance. Herbs such as black cohosh, dong quai and red clover are often used to help manage menopausal symptoms.
